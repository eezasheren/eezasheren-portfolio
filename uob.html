The Problem Space

Designing inside constraints most designers never see

Corporate banking UX isn't hard because the interfaces are complex. It's hard because everything around them is.

UOB Infinity serves corporate clients across Southeast Asia, treasurers, finance managers, operational teams. Every decision sits at the intersection of regulatory requirements, security controls, legacy systems, and user habits built over years.

No blank canvas. Getting it wrong has real financial and legal consequences. That context shapes everything.

My Role

Not just a screen-maker, a thinking partner

Embedded in a live product team. 8 people, multiple concurrent initiatives. The role was never just about producing screens.

My value was helping teams get clearer on the right problem before jumping to solutions. Asking uncomfortable questions in refinements, flagging when a quick fix had usability implications nobody spotted, holding the line when business pressure pushed the other way.

I owned end-to-end design on several key workstreams and contributed across others, from problem framing through to handoff.

How I Work

Four things that define how I operate

01 / Agile Delivery

Ship fast. Don't cut corners.

The skill isn't speed, it's knowing what needs to be right before a sprint ends and what can wait. I make that call quickly and communicate it clearly. Fast without sacrificing quality.

02 / Product Thinking

Start with the problem, not the brief.

The most valuable thing a designer can do isn't design the solution, it's challenge whether you're solving the right problem. I regularly reframed requests before opening Figma. That pushback either sharpened the brief or saved the team from building the wrong thing entirely.

03 / Systems & Security

Constraints are the brief.

Compliance, security protocols, legacy systems. Most designers never encounter these. I stopped treating them as blockers early on. The real craft is making something feel simple despite everything underneath. Constraints don't kill creativity, they shape it.

04 / Cross-Functional

Design as a shared language.

Daily work with POs, BAs, engineers, and senior stakeholders, each with a different definition of "good." My job was translation. Sometimes a quick sketch in a meeting does more than a polished Figma file. Getting everyone to see the same problem is half the work.

01 / 04

Making high-stakes payments feel less frightening

UOB Pay / UOB Send

The Problem

Corporate users moving significant sums across borders are under pressure. The existing flows created friction at the exact moments they needed the most confidence.

How I Approached It

I mapped the journey to find where trust broke down, not just usability. Where did users second-guess themselves? Where did language create ambiguity? That analysis drove prioritisation.

What I Did

Redesigned confirmation patterns, status communication, and decision architecture across the payment flow. Every change had to survive compliance and technical review.

Key Learning

In high-stakes flows, clarity beats cleverness every time.

02 / 04

Untangling a mental model nobody had mapped

Virtual Account Sub-Account (VASA)

The Problem

Corporate clients managing complex account hierarchies, parent accounts, sub-accounts, multiple currencies, had no intuitive way to understand what they owned or how to navigate it.

How I Approached It

Before touching the UI, I focused on the mental model. How do these users think about account structures? The challenge was representational, making invisible hierarchy visible without overwhelming the screen.

What I Did

Designed the full sub-account experience, hierarchy visualisation, state management, flow architecture. Built closely with engineering around the actual data model.

Key Learning

The gap between how a system is built and how users understand it is where the real UX work lives.

03 / 04

The unglamorous work that actually makes a product better

Agile Product Enhancements

The Problem

Business teams regularly needed urgent UX changes within short sprint timelines. Volume was high, timelines were tight, and quality still had to hold.

How I Approached It

For every request, one quick check: does this serve the user, or just satisfy the ask? Often I'd land on something slightly different that did both. Sometimes I had to deliver what was asked. Knowing the difference mattered.

What I Did

Triaged and shipped 28+ UX improvements across live product flows, from minor refinements to full flow redesigns within sprint constraints.

Key Learning

Taste and speed don't have to be opposites. The skill is knowing what "good enough" means in context.

04 / 04

Designing for people who can't afford to miss things

Fraud Detection & Risk Monitoring (OFM FDR)

The Problem

Internal teams monitoring fraud and risk were scanning dense data under pressure, making time-sensitive calls. The tools gave them information, not clarity.

How I Approached It

I reframed it as a decision-support problem. Not "how do we show more data?" but "what does this person need to know in the next 90 seconds?" That shift changed the entire design direction.

What I Did

Designed the dashboard with a clear hierarchy based on urgency and decision-relevance. Worked directly with ops teams to understand how they actually worked, not just what they said they needed.

Key Learning

The best internal tools are invisible. When the interface stops being something people think about, you've done your job.

What it added up to

Two years. Real constraints, real consequences. Work that's used daily across Southeast Asia.

I learned to hold the line on quality under pressure. Great UX at enterprise scale isn't one brilliant solution, it's hundreds of small, considered decisions made consistently over time.

Reflection

The best design work is invisible. No one notices the confirmation pattern you agonised over, they just feel more confident. That's the job.

The real skill isn't designing within constraints, it's making something feel effortless despite everything underneath. That tension is what I find most interesting about this work.

If I were starting over

I'd document design decisions as we went, not just Figma files but the rationale. Knowledge lives in people's heads. A decision log would have made every handoff smoother.
